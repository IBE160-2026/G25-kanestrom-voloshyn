# Photosynthesis: Study Notes

## 1. Overview

Photosynthesis is the process in which plants, algae, and some bacteria turn light energy into chemical energy stored in sugar. It takes place in the chloroplasts, which are small organelles found mainly in the cells of the leaf mesophyll. A single mesophyll cell can contain between 30 and 40 chloroplasts.

The overall reaction can be summarised as six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water, using light energy, giving one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen. The oxygen released into the air comes from the water molecules, not from the carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s with experiments that used water labelled with the heavy oxygen isotope O-18.

Photosynthesis has two linked stages. The light-dependent reactions capture light energy and store it in two carrier molecules, ATP and NADPH. The Calvin cycle then uses ATP and NADPH to build sugar from carbon dioxide. The first stage happens in the thylakoid membranes, and the second stage happens in the stroma, the fluid that surrounds the thylakoids.

## 2. Pigments and light

The main pigment is chlorophyll a. Chlorophyll b and the carotenoids are accessory pigments that absorb other wavelengths and pass the energy on to chlorophyll a. Carotenoids also protect the chloroplast by getting rid of excess energy that would otherwise damage it.

Chlorophyll absorbs blue light (around 430 nm) and red light (around 660 nm) most strongly. It reflects green light, which is why leaves look green. A graph of how much light a pigment absorbs at each wavelength is called an absorption spectrum. A graph of how fast photosynthesis runs at each wavelength is called an action spectrum. The two graphs have a similar shape, which supports the idea that chlorophyll drives the process.

In autumn, many trees break down their chlorophyll and move its nitrogen back into the trunk and roots. The yellow and orange carotenoids, which were there all summer, then become visible.

## 3. The light-dependent reactions

The light-dependent reactions use two protein complexes called photosystems. Photosystem II comes first in the chain, even though it was discovered second. Its reaction centre is called P680, after the wavelength of light it absorbs best. The reaction centre of Photosystem I is called P700.

When light excites P680, it passes a high-energy electron to an electron transport chain. To replace that electron, Photosystem II splits water. This step, called photolysis, releases oxygen gas and hydrogen ions into the thylakoid space.

As electrons move along the transport chain, their energy pumps more hydrogen ions into the thylakoid space. The ions then flow back out to the stroma through an enzyme called ATP synthase, which uses the flow to make ATP. This way of making ATP is called chemiosmosis.

At Photosystem I, light excites the electrons again. They finally reduce NADP+ to NADPH. In total, the light-dependent reactions produce ATP, NADPH, and oxygen, and they use water and light.

## 4. The Calvin cycle

The Calvin cycle is named after Melvin Calvin, who worked out its steps in the 1950s and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1961. It does not use light directly, but it depends on the ATP and NADPH that light produces, so in most plants it stops soon after dark.

The cycle has three phases:

1. **Carbon fixation.** The enzyme rubisco attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar called RuBP. The result splits at once into two three-carbon molecules called 3-PGA.
2. **Reduction.** ATP and NADPH turn 3-PGA into a three-carbon sugar called G3P.
3. **Regeneration.** Most of the G3P is used, with more ATP, to rebuild RuBP so the cycle can continue.

For every three turns of the cycle, three carbon dioxide molecules are fixed and one G3P molecule leaves the cycle. Two G3P molecules are needed to make one glucose molecule. The plant uses G3P to make glucose, sucrose for transport, starch for storage, and cellulose for cell walls.

Rubisco is thought to be the most common protein on Earth. It is also slow and makes mistakes: it sometimes attaches oxygen instead of carbon dioxide. This side reaction is called photorespiration, and it wastes energy and releases carbon that was already fixed.

## 5. Limiting factors

The rate of photosynthesis depends on light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature. At any moment, the factor in shortest supply limits the rate. This is called the law of limiting factors, described by Frederick Blackman in 1905.

- **Light intensity.** The rate rises with light until another factor becomes limiting. Very strong light can damage the photosystems.
- **Carbon dioxide.** Air contains about 0.04 % carbon dioxide. Commercial greenhouses often raise it to around 0.1 % to increase yield.
- **Temperature.** The Calvin cycle is driven by enzymes, so the rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, often around 25 °C for temperate plants, and then falls as the enzymes are damaged.

The light compensation point is the light intensity at which photosynthesis exactly balances respiration, so the plant neither gains nor loses sugar.

## 6. C4 and CAM plants

In hot, dry conditions, plants close their stomata to save water. Carbon dioxide inside the leaf then runs low, and rubisco performs more photorespiration. Two adaptations reduce this problem.

**C4 plants**, such as maize and sugar cane, first fix carbon dioxide in the mesophyll cells with an enzyme called PEP carboxylase, which never binds oxygen. The product is a four-carbon acid, which gives the group its name. This acid is moved to bundle-sheath cells deep in the leaf, where it releases carbon dioxide at a high concentration for rubisco. The two steps are separated in space.

**CAM plants**, such as cacti and pineapple, open their stomata only at night. They store carbon dioxide as malic acid in large vacuoles and release it to the Calvin cycle during the day, when the stomata are closed. The two steps are separated in time.

## 7. Photosynthesis and cellular respiration

Cellular respiration is roughly the reverse of photosynthesis: it breaks glucose down with oxygen to release energy as ATP, giving carbon dioxide and water. Plants carry out both processes. Respiration runs day and night in the mitochondria, while photosynthesis runs only in light in the chloroplasts.

| | Photosynthesis | Cellular respiration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organelle | Chloroplast | Mitochondrion |
| Uses | Carbon dioxide, water, light | Glucose, oxygen |
| Produces | Glucose, oxygen | Carbon dioxide, water, ATP |
| When | Only in light | All the time |

## 8. Key terms

- **Chloroplast:** the organelle where photosynthesis happens.
- **Thylakoid:** a membrane sac inside the chloroplast; site of the light-dependent reactions.
- **Stroma:** the fluid around the thylakoids; site of the Calvin cycle.
- **Photolysis:** the splitting of water by Photosystem II, which releases oxygen.
- **Rubisco:** the enzyme that fixes carbon dioxide in the Calvin cycle.
- **Photorespiration:** the wasteful reaction when rubisco binds oxygen instead of carbon dioxide.
- **Limiting factor:** the factor in shortest supply, which sets the rate of the process.
